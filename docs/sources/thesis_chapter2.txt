Chapter 2
Literature Review
The increasing use of deep learning in autonomous vehicles (AVs) has improved perception
and decision-making, but it has also introduced security vulnerabilities into safety-critical
driving systems. This chapter reviews the literature in a sequence that moves from the
broad security of autonomous driving to the specific problem addressed in this thesis. It
first discusses lane diversion and its security challenges, then examines backdoor attacks
targeting lane diversion models, with particular attention to naturalistic and diffusion-
based data poisoning. It next reviews backdoor attacks affecting other autonomous-
driving tasks and vision-language or foundation models, followed by physical and system-
level security threats and existing defence and detection approaches. Finally, the chapter
synthesizes these findings to identify the research gap and summarize the studies most
relevant to developing an efficient, task-specific diffusion model for naturalistic backdoor
poisoning in lane diversion systems [?,?,?].
2.1 Security of Deep Learning in Autonomous Driv-
ing
Among these components, lane diversion is particularly important because its outputs
can directly influence path planning and steering decisions. In benchmark datasets like
TuSimple and CULane, deep-learning approaches for lane diversion have made significant
progress, yet good performance on benchmarks doesn’t guarantee performance in real-
world driving situations. Sato and Chen [?] performed a detailed analysis of physical-
world adversarial attacks against lane diversion models and demonstrated that standard
metrics of lane diversion might be inadequate for evaluating a model’s robustness in
an end-to-end autonomous driving context. Their findings showed that the results of
a model that is performing well in conventional evaluation can be sensitive when the
model’s prediction is applied to the actual vehicle control.
Recently, MohajerAnsari et al. [?] showed that by applying naturally occurring neg-

2.2 Physical and Adversarial Vulnerabilities in Lane
Diversion
Backdoor attacks are a particularly important threat because they compromise a model
during the training stage rather than requiring an attacker to manipulate every inference
input. A typical backdoor attack involves the attacker changing a small proportion of the
training data by inserting a trigger and associating the triggered sample with a malicious
target behaviour. The result is that the model will perform normally if fed a clean input,
but if there is a trigger in the input, it will generate an attacker-controlled output. There
are four categories of back-door attacks in general surveys of back-door learning: based
on triggers, poisoning methods, target task, and attack stage [?,?,?,?,?,?].
The first papers that explicitly focused on the problem of lane diversion showed that
lane detection models can also be poisoned during training time. A physical-world back-
door attack, BadLANE, was proposed by Zhang et al. [?] specifically for lane diversion.
BadLANE’s digital backdoor trigger is not of a fixed shape, position, and viewpoint that
digital triggers are usually required to have, but rather it is an amorphous trigger pattern
that can mimic mud or pollution on the surface of a road or camera lens. The approach
also incorporates some meta-learning to tune the triggers to different environmental con-
ditions like weather and lighting. The effectiveness of the proposed attack was shown
under different conditions by performing experiments on various lane diversion models in
digital and physical environments.
For the lane diversion attack, Liao et al. [?] introduced DBALD, a stealthy data poi-
soning method in the naturalistic setting. The aim of DBALD is to produce poisoned
samples that look realistic with the appearance and semantics of the original driving
scene. The method uses diffusion-based image editing together with heatmap-guided
trigger placement and losses designed to preserve both lane information and the sur-
rounding environment. The resulting poisoned samples should be hard for humans to
detect without reducing the probability of a successful attack while maintaining a high
attack success rate and clean performance.
DBALD is a significant advance in backdoor lane-diversion research that moves be-

whether task-specific diffusion distillation can reduce naturalistic trigger-generation cost
while retaining attack effectiveness.
2.3 Naturalistic and Diffusion-Based Data Poisoning
The purpose of naturalistic data poisoning is to hide the nature of a trigger by ensuring
that the poisoned training samples appear like the samples in their natural environment.
Naturalistic triggers are more like a familiar object or element of the environment that
might be seen during driving and are different from conventional square patches or ar-
tificial patterns. BadLANE has shown that lane diversion systems can be effectively
triggered by physical patterns in the form of mud [?]. Building on this, DBALD went in
a new direction with diffusion-based image editing to produce naturalistic triggers that
retain the visual attributes of the original image [?].
Diffusion models can be used for high-quality image generation and editing. The
drawback of general-purpose diffusion models is that they are costly due to the fact that
generally multiple denoising steps are needed in order to generate an image. Conventional
trigger-generation approaches are reported to be more expensive than DBALD by a fac-
tor of the number of images per generation according to DBALD’s authors, and they
believe that there is a potential direction of research to improve the cost-effectiveness
of DBALD: a distilled diffusion model tailored for the specific task [?]. This observa-
tion is relevant to the current research. In contrast to a common diffusion model for
all trigger-generation operations, a Task-Specific Model could potentially learn the spe-
cific transformation needed for the naturalistic poisoning of autonomous-driving images.
The Task-Specific Model would focus on a narrow task and may generate realistic poison
samples by conducting fewer denoising steps. The cited naturalistic and diffusion-based
lane-diversion work motivates this efficiency question, but does not establish the perfor-
mance of a distilled, lane-diversion-specific generator.

2.4 Trajectory Prediction, 3D Perception, and Trig-
ger Placement
The backdoor approach has also been expanded to other components of autonomous
driving. The threat is shown in these studies not to be limited to the problem of lane
diversion and has the potential to impact prediction, decision-making, 3D perception,
and multimodal systems.
Pourkeshavarz et al. [?] advanced an attack on trajectory prediction models that
was more naturalistic. They employ a method of doing training-time poisoning with
naturalistic samples generated by changing the trajectory of an attacking vehicle and
transforming the scene. The attack is intended to be effective even if there is no deep
knowledge about the victim model and was tested on several trajectory prediction mod-
els and datasets. The analysis in this study shows how a stealthy attack could impact
the prediction phase of the autonomous vehicle pipeline, directly impacting the motion
planning phase. Chen et al. [?] proposed a temporal-logic-based backdoor attack for
end-to-end autonomous driving (E2E AD) via offline reinforcement learning (RL) agents.
Their method is rather than using a traditional image patch to trigger, they use the tra-
jectory of one or more other cars. Temporal logic specifications are employed to precisely
specify trigger behaviours, and the addition of negative training samples increases stealth-
iness. Trajectory-based triggers have been shown to result in malicious driving actions
in experiments with several offline reinforcement learning agents. Although these stud-
ies demonstrate backdoor risks in prediction and decision-making, they do not address
efficient diffusion-based generation of poisoned images for lane-diversion models.
Backdoor attacks have also been explored with regard to 3D perception. One of
the first works studying backdoor attacks on LiDAR object detection in autonomous
driving, Zhang et al. [?] presented. They poison a small amount of samples in the
point cloud and trigger with physically realizable objects. They also proposed a stealthy
poisoning method, where the injected points are hidden in the fake vehicle point clusters.
The work demonstrates that training-time backdoors can have an impact on perception
models beyond images, such as point-cloud models. Guo et al. [?] presented a 6DoF pose
estimation backdoor attack, 6DAttack. The idea is to make use of object pose information
for the creation of inconspicuous triggers for 3D perception systems. This study also
shows that backdoor attacks are increasingly moving from 2D image classification to
more complex spatial perception tasks.
Jo and Cho [?] studied how the location of the trigger in a backdoor attack against
a deep learning object detection system affects its performance. They proposed a Back-
door Trigger Position Search Algorithm which seeks trigger positions rather than fixed
positions that are considered as triggers. Their findings show that the location of trig-
gers can significantly impact on the success of the attack. Although their method is not

2.5 Vision-Language and Foundation Models
Large multimodal and foundation models have been developed quickly, making autonomous
systems’ attack surface even larger. The vision-language models (VLMs) integrate visual
data and language-based reasoning, and are being studied extensively for autonomous
driving use cases. Multimodal nature adds other potential hidden triggers.
The work of Ni et al. [?] showed that autonomous driving systems that rely on vision-
language models can exhibit unsafe behaviour in the presence of physical backdoor trig-
gers. They found that physical objects in the surrounding environment of the car can
trigger malicious behavior, but still provide normal driving functions in the absence of the
trigger. Liu et al. [?] proposed a natural reflection backdoor attack on the autonomous
driving VLM. The method is not an artificial patch, but rather natural reflections within
the environment used as hidden triggers. The type of trigger where there is a reflection
is especially challenging to detect because reflections are very common in real driving
scenarios and thus may not be alarming to human viewers.
Zhong et al. [?] presented a stealthy semantic backdoor attack against VLMs. They
take a different approach from direct visual cues and make use of the semantic informa-
tion in other modalities. The attacks show that such a model can be attacked even if
there is no apparent visual input trigger. Wang et al. [?] also introduced a multimodal
backdoor attack for autonomous driving VLMs by using naturalistic graffiti synthesized
by diffusion-based image inpainting and a cross-lingual text trigger. In their experiments
on DriveVLM, they showed that normal performance in clean tasks can be maintained
even when naturalistic visual triggers are added to the multimodal backdoor attack.
This work is important to the current research as it further illustrates the feasibility of
diffusion-based image generation as a way to produce naturalistic backdoor triggers.
Other foundation models, not just VLMs, have been subject to backdoor attacks.
Zhang et al. [?] conducted a study on attacks to prompt-based video segmentation foun-
dation models. They have shown it is possible to embed hidden triggers in prompt and
video inputs to influence segmentation outcomes. In combination, the studies suggest
that backdoor attacks have evolved in line with the modern foundation models and are
not limited to traditional convolutional neural networks. A recent example of the growing
pace of the development of large-scale vision-language foundation models is the model
Qwen3-VL [?]. While the technical report is not a study of a backdoor attack, the growing
capability and deployment of such multimodal architectures highlight the need to assess
the security of such systems before their use in safety-critical autonomous driving appli-

2.6 Sensor-Fusion, Localization, and Mapping At-
tacks
There are multiple system and physical attacks that are possible against autonomous driv-
ing systems, apart from the training-time backdoor attacks. These studies are relevant
since they have shown the impact of perception errors on downstream vehicle behaviour.
Tehrani et al. [?] proposed a taxonomy of system-level attacks to autonomous vehicles
deep learning models. They looked at a vast amount of research and classified attacks
based on the attacked component, the attack mechanism, the threat model, and the
propagation of model-level errors to system-level failures. Jing et al. [?] studied physical
attacks on lane diversion and showed that with proper road marking design, false lane
structures can be generated, leading a real vehicle to steer in the wrong direction. Sato
and Chen [?] also demonstrated that the evaluation of lane diversion robustness needs
to be performed in realistic physical environments, not just by conventional benchmark
measures.
In the study by MohajerAnsari et al. [?], the investigators examined negative shad-
ows as hidden physical attacks towards lane diversion systems. They found that this
is enough: They can make artificial lane-like shadow patterns using the manipulation
of sunlight, thus showing that the diversion of the lanes can be influenced by natural-
looking environmental changes. Other autonomous vehicle perception components have
also proven to be vulnerable. Proposed by Ma et al. [?], ControlLoc is an adversarial
patch that consists of a physical patch that can be placed in the environment to influence
camera-based perception and object tracking. The attack was assessed in real-world vehi-
cle experiments, demonstrating that attack propagation from the perception level to the
vehicle level could be performed. However, in the context of LiDAR-based perception,
Sato et al. [?] examined the realism of LiDAR spoofing attacks at high speed and long
range, complementing previous attacks which were mostly conducted in a controlled envi-
ronment. Jin et al. [?] proposed PhantomLiDAR, which uses electromagnetic interference
to control the LiDAR measurements through cross-modality signal injection. In Shadow
Hack [?] Kobayashi et al. introduced a technique that uses shadows and materials that
are hard for LiDAR to accurately measure.
Multi-sensor systems are also susceptible to attacks. Zhu et al. [?] explored the ma-
licious attacks on LiDAR-camera-radar systems. Their findings show that sensor fusion
is not a direct solution for guaranteeing security, as simultaneous manipulation of the
inputs of different sensors can still impact the final perception output. Zhang et al. [?]

investigated data fabrication attacks on collaborative vehicular perception. In such a
system, vehicles share perception data with other vehicles, thereby establishing a further
trust boundary. The authors demonstrated that adversarial cars can manipulate percep-
tion data and affect the actions of other cars. Other attack surfaces have emerged in
localization and mapping. Zhang et al. [?] analyzed the vulnerabilities of multi-sensor-
fusion localization and showed that both physical attacks and GPS attacks can affect the
localization accuracy. Lou et al. [?] have investigated the exploitation of online HD map
construction and demonstrated that structural weaknesses in the map prediction process
can be exploited via physical interference and camera-based attacks. Taken together,
these studies address physical, sensor, collaborative-perception, or localization threats;
they do not investigate the computational efficiency of diffusion-based training-time trig-
ger generation for lane diversion.
2.7 Defences Against Attacks on Autonomous-Driving
Models
While many studies have suggested new backdoor attacks, only a few studies have been
dedicated to reliable detection and defence. The current defenses can be classified into
data-level, model-level, trigger reverse engineering, input-level, and robustness-based de-
fenses.
Li et al. [?] and Jin et al. [?] offer comprehensive surveys of backdoor detection
and defense methods. Hanif et al. [?] have updated the taxonomy for trigger construc-
tion, poisoning strategies, model-level attacks, computer-vision backdoors, and defense
techniques. Abbasi et al. [?] and Jin et al. [?] have updated the taxonomy of trigger
construction, poisoning strategies, model-level attacks, computer-vision backdoors, and
defense techniques. More specifically, Zhao et al. [?] studied the data poisoning attacks
against deep learning and classified the attacks based on their goal, attack method, and
target scenarios. The proposed work in this thesis is relevant to the present research,
as it examines a training-time poisoning process and tries to efficiently generate realistic
poisoned samples.
Kim et. al. [?] introduced an adversarial patch-feature energy method for defending
against physical adversarial attacks in object detection systems. This work shows the
benefits of defending features at the level of physical perturbations, but it isn’t specifi-
cally tailored for efficient generation of training-time naturalistic poison samples. Liu et
al. [?] studied quantized delta-weight methods for maintaining safety in model adapta-
tion. While not a backdoor way to generate triggers, their work shows how the concept
of efficiency and safety extends to modifying and adapting deep learning models in gen-
eral. In the physical autonomous vehicle attack, Liu et al. [?] proposed a defense scheme

using motion planning to defend against adversarial object-based attacks. Similarly,
Zhang et al. [?] proposed a real-time defense against object-based LiDAR attacks. These
methods are useful as they show that defensive mechanisms can act at various stages of
the autonomous vehicles pipeline. But they primarily focus on inference-time physical
attacks, not the computational task of creating naturalistic poisoned training samples.
Accordingly, the reviewed defence and model-adaptation studies do not directly resolve
the specific generation-efficiency problem considered in this thesis.
2.8 Comparative Summary of the Reviewed Studies
The reviewed state-of-the-art approaches address backdoor threats in autonomous-driving
systems through different attack strategies, trigger-generation methods, and defence
mechanisms. Lane-diversion-focused studies, such as BadLANE and DBALD, demon-
strate how poisoned training samples can induce targeted changes in lane predictions,
while naturalistic and diffusion-based approaches aim to make triggers more visually
plausible and less conspicuous. Other studies investigate backdoors in trajectory pre-
diction, 3D perception, vision-language models, and sensor-fusion systems, illustrating
that the threat extends beyond lane-related tasks. Existing defences attempt to identify
or mitigate poisoned data and compromised models, but their effectiveness depends on
the attack setting, trigger characteristics, and model architecture. Compared with these
approaches, the central opportunity identified in this thesis is to improve the efficiency
of naturalistic trigger generation specifically for lane-diversion scenarios. In particular,
a task-specific distilled diffusion model could reduce the computational cost and infer-
ence steps associated with general-purpose diffusion-based generation while seeking to
preserve trigger realism and attack effectiveness. Thus, the reviewed studies establish the
feasibility and diversity of backdoor attacks in autonomous driving, while also motivating
further investigation into efficient, driving-scene-oriented trigger generation.
BadLANE and DBALD are the most directly related studies to the present the-
sis. BadLANE demonstrates physical-world lane-diversion backdoor poisoning using
road- or lens-like contamination triggers, whereas DBALD advances naturalistic poi-
soning through diffusion-based image editing and lane-aware trigger placement. DBALD
is therefore the closest baseline for this work. Its use of a general-purpose diffusion
backbone motivates investigating whether a task-specific distilled generator can reduce
trigger-generation cost while retaining visual realism and attack effectiveness. This effi-
ciency improvement is a research objective of the present thesis, not an established result
of the reviewed studies.

2.9 Research Gap
Although recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of backdoor attacks against
lane diversion systems, existing approaches still face challenges in generating naturalistic
and computationally efficient poisoned samples. Diffusion-based methods can produce
realistic triggers, but relying on general-purpose, high-capacity diffusion models may
introduce substantial generation time and computational overhead. These models are
not necessarily optimized for the visual characteristics and constraints of driving scenes.
Therefore, a research gap remains in developing a task-specific diffusion approach that can
generate realistic lane-diversion triggers with reduced computational cost while preserving
the effectiveness and stealthiness of the backdoor attack.
2.10 Problem Statement
The problem addressed in this thesis is the computational expense of generating natural-
istic poisoned samples for backdoor attacks on lane diversion models. Existing diffusion-
based approaches may require considerable inference time and computational resources
when using general-purpose diffusion models to create driving-scene triggers. This over-
head can limit the practical scalability of poisoning experiments. The research problem is
therefore to reduce the computational cost and generation time of diffusion-based trigger
creation without substantially compromising the visual realism of the generated samples
or the effectiveness of the resulting backdoor attack.
2.11 Proposed Solution
To address this problem, this thesis proposes a task-specific distilled diffusion model for
generating naturalistic triggers in driving scenes for backdoor poisoning of lane diversion
models. The proposed approach aims to adapt the generation process to the visual
characteristics of autonomous-driving environments and reduce the number of diffusion
inference steps required to produce poisoned samples. By distilling knowledge from a
pre-trained general-purpose diffusion model into a more efficient model, the approach
seeks to reduce trigger-generation time and computational overhead while maintaining
